The first conversation with a client after they sign a mandate sets the tone for everything after it. Walk in with a job description and a promise to “start researching”, and you spend the next month catching up. Walk in with a picture of the market, and the client starts making decisions with you on day one.

A market map does not need to be finished to be useful at kickoff. It needs to be honest about its scope, show that the obvious names have been considered, and give the client something concrete to react to. What follows is a method for getting there in the days between the signature and the first working session.

## Start from the role, not the company

Before a single company name, write down what the role actually needs: the scale of the business the person must have run, the ownership model they’ve worked under, the markets they know. A Group CFO for a listed energy utility and a CFO for a founder-owned renewables developer share a title and almost nothing else. The brief is the filter.

Three questions do most of the work:

- **Scale.** What size of organisation, measured in headcount or revenue, has this person already led at the level the role demands?
- **Context.** Listed or private, group or division, turnaround or growth: which situations are transferable and which are not?
- **Geography.** Where must the person be able to work from, and which markets must they already know?

Write the answers into the brief before you open a database. They will be the criteria you filter on, and they are the first thing the client will want to challenge. Getting that challenge early is the point.

## Draw the universe wide

Translate the brief into criteria a database understands — industry, headcount band, revenue band, headquarters region — and pull everything that matches. Resist the urge to pre-judge. A universe that is too narrow on day one is the reason a search stalls in week six.

A wide first pull does two things. It surfaces the adjacent sectors a client did not think to mention — the utility whose trading arm looks like a commodities house, the engineering group that has quietly become an energy business. And it gives you a denominator. “We looked at every company in DACH with more than a thousand employees in these three industries” is a far stronger statement than a list of twenty names, however good the names are.

Save the search that produced the universe. Criteria change as the client reacts, and being able to show what the universe looked like before and after a change is part of defending the eventual shortlist.

## Decide on every company

Every company in the universe gets one of three answers: it stays in the universe, it’s shortlisted, or it’s declined. The declines matter as much as the shortlist. When the client asks “what about the obvious competitor?”, the answer is already written down.

> A declined company is research, not waste. Recording the no is what makes the yes defensible.

Treat the three stages as decisions, not as a to-do list:

1. **In universe** means the company matches the criteria and has not yet been judged. It is where every company starts.
2. **Shortlisted** means you would put people from this company in front of the client. Say why in a sentence.
3. **Declined** means you looked and decided against it — too small, wrong ownership model, an off-limits client of your own firm. Record the reason while you still remember it.

Declining in bulk is fine. If forty companies fail the same criterion, decline them together with the same reason. What matters is that no company in the universe is left without a decision by the time you present.

## Map people, not just logos

For each shortlisted company, name the executives who could plausibly do the job — the incumbent in the role, the deputy, the finance director of the largest division. One company with three credible people is worth more than three companies with none, and the map should show it.

Keep people attached to the company they sit in, and keep a slot open for companies where you have not found anyone yet. An empty slot is useful information: it tells the client where the research is still thin, and it tells your researchers where to look next.

Not every executive worth mapping works at a company in your universe. Someone who left a target company last year, or who runs a division of a business outside your filters, still belongs on the map. Add them against the mandate rather than forcing them into a company that does not fit.

Bring in what your team already knows. Past candidate lists, a colleague’s spreadsheet from a similar search, and the profile you happened to be reading this morning are all research. The discipline is to get them into one place, attached to the right company, rather than scattered across files and tabs.

## Walk in with a map

Show the client where the market is: which countries, which companies, which people. Geography answers relocation and compensation questions before they’re asked, and a shared map turns the kickoff into a working session instead of a status update.

Structure the session around decisions the client can make on the spot:

- **Confirm the universe.** Walk through the criteria and the size of the pull. Ask what is missing.
- **Challenge the declines.** Show the companies you ruled out and why. This is where clients add the most, often by telling you which competitors are genuinely off limits.
- **React to the shortlist.** Show the shortlisted companies and the people mapped at each. Ask which profiles feel closest to what they have in mind.

Leave with changes to the criteria, a list of companies to revisit, and a clearer picture of the person the client is actually looking for. That is a better outcome than a polished list, and it is only possible because the market was mapped before the call rather than after it.

The method is not complicated. Write the brief first, draw the universe wide, decide on every company, attach people to places, and show the client the whole picture. What makes it work is doing it in the first week, while the client’s attention is highest and every decision is still cheap to change.
